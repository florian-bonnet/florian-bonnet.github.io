Research themes

My research program is structured around four interconnected lines of research at the intersection of demography, economics, history and spatial analysis. They converge on a central question: how do spatial inequalities in lifespan and economic development evolve over the long run, and what drives their divergence or convergence?

Of course, research is inherently a collaborative endeavour, and the work I present here would not have been possible without my colleagues. You will find their names in the list of publications.

1. Long-run spatial inequalities in longevity in France

I reconstruct regional life tables for 90 French departments, building one of the longest continuous subnational demographic series in Europe. This work has produced annual lifetables by sex and year (1901–2023). I also reconstruct lifetables for Paris and its 20 districts by sex and year (1872–2019), along with indicators on cause-of-death structures for Paris and its 80 neighbourhoods (1888–1949). Drawing on these data, I have documented the long period of convergence in regional life expectancy that France experienced between the late 19th century and the second half of the 20th century, as well as the gradual divergence observed since. I have also shown that the concept of an urban penalty did not apply uniformly to all Parisian districts at the end of the 19th century. Finally, the analysis of causes of death has allowed me to better characterise the epidemiological transition that took place in Paris during the first half of the 20th century.

Key publications: PDR 2020 (Convergence and divergence between French regions), Demographic Research 2020 (French Human Mortality Database paper). PDR 2025 (Parisian epidemiological transition)

2. European regional longevity divergences since 1990

I document and explain the divergent longevity trajectories of European regions since the fall of the Berlin wall. This work has first allowed me to compare the situations in France and Germany, the latter showing a rapid divergence in recent years. I have also contributed to two large-scale European studies showing that this divergence has been visible across the European space since the mid-2000s, and that it partly accounts for the recent slowdown in life expectancy gains in Europe. Lagging regions no longer converge, and their life expectancy gains are now close to zero. Future research will need to investigate the causes of this phenomenon.

Key publications: Nature Communications 2026 (European deceleration in life expectancy gains), PDR 2024 (European divergence in longevity), Population Health Metrics 2024 (French-German comparison).

3. Health crises, pandemics and climate-related mortality

Alongside my work on long-term demographic dynamics, I am also interested in the extreme events that have disrupted the long march towards higher life expectancy. This has led me to study the most recent major crisis, the Covid-19 pandemic, from both a French and an international perspective, including estimates of regional excess mortality across Europe. I have also examined earlier crises, such as the 1911 and 2003 heatwaves, and the Spanish flu (1918–19) and Hong Kong flu (1968–69) pandemics. My recent research in this area aims to precisely disentangle the Covid-19 shock from long-term dynamics, and to draw on fine-grained data to estimate excess mortality from extreme heat events in the Mediterranean region.

Key publications: PLoS ONE 2024 (Subnational excess mortality methods). Nature Communications 2024 (COVID-19 across 569 European regions), PDR 2025 (COVID-19 vs historical crises), Demographic Research 2022 (Hong-Kong flu)

4. Regional economic development in Europe

My research includes the estimation of economic development data at a fine-grained regional scale. I have reconstructed per capita income levels for the departments of metropolitan France since 1922, based on tax statistics from the personal income tax. In a similar vein, I have reconstructed income distributions for French departments since 1960. These data have shown that regional inequalities declined substantially between 1922 and the late 20th century, but have been rising again since the early 2000s, driven by an increasingly rapid spatial polarisation of the wealthiest households. Combined with mortality data, they have also revealed that the richest regions today are those with the highest life expectancy, in contrast with the first half of the 20th century. Future research will aim to reconstruct regional income distributions for as many European countries as possible, and to exploit newly available tax statistics to build historical indicators of economic development.

Key publications: Journal of Economic Geography 2026 (High-income spatial concentration), Economics and Statistics 2021 (100 years of French regional income convergence), Plos One 2023 (Economic regional gradient in longevity).
